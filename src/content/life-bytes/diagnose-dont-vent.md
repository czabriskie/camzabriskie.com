I've been building ClimbIQ for a while now, a side project that tries to help people figure out what's actually missing between their resume and the jobs they're going for, and part of building it means I end up reading a lot of the posts people write about their own job search. There's a pattern that shows up constantly, something like "I've been out of work for eight months, sent out a thousand applications, haven't heard back from almost any of them." I get why people write it that way, it's true and it's exhausting and it deserves to be said. But I've started thinking it's also kind of the worst possible way to ask for help, even when help is exactly what someone wants.

The problem with "a thousand applications, nothing" is that it doesn't actually give anyone reading it something to do. It's not really a question, it's more just a status update on how bad things are, and the only honest response to a status update is sympathy, which doesn't get anyone hired. I started wondering what it would look like if the same person said something closer to, I've been out this long, I went and compared the postings I care about against my actual experience, and I noticed these five things I'm consistently missing. I've done this and this to start closing a couple of them, and I've updated my resume so it actually speaks to what I have instead of what I don't.

That's a completely different post to read. It's not more optimistic, the person might still be just as stuck, but now there's something concrete in it. A hiring manager, a mentor, a random connection scrolling past, all of them suddenly have something to respond to that isn't just "I'm sorry, that's rough." Maybe someone actually knows how to close one of those five gaps. Maybe someone just says that resume line is fine, actually, the real problem is somewhere else. Either way the conversation has somewhere to go.

Same thing applies to a bad interview, I think. "That interview went badly" doesn't invite anything back. "That interview went badly because I couldn't clearly explain a project I led two years ago, and I've been working on tightening that story before the next one" invites someone to actually help tighten it, or tell you it's not the story that was the problem in the first place.

I don't think this is really about tone or forced positivity, people can see through fake optimism from a mile away and it doesn't help either. It's more that diagnosing your own situation, even partially, even when you're not sure you've got it exactly right, does something a raw complaint can't. It turns you from someone waiting on the market into someone who's already working the problem, which happens to be a much easier person to want to help. I keep going back and forth on whether that's obvious advice everyone already knows and just doesn't do under stress, or whether it's actually a framing nobody's told people to try. Probably somewhere in between.
