---
title: "Checking the math"
description: "Verifying an AI's dosing math against a bottle label taught me the difference between roughly right and exactly right."
date: 2026-09-07T13:00:00Z
---

We're filling water jugs for emergency storage, 23 of them at 3.5 gallons each, and part of doing that right is adding a preserver that's supposed to stretch how long stored water stays good, from something like six months to five years according to the product. I haven't verified that five-years number myself, and I still want to go look into what the stuff is actually doing to the water chemically, but for now I just needed to get the dose right.

The bottle says 8 drops per gallon. Do that across 23 jugs at 3.5 gallons each and you're well past 600 drops, and I was not going to count drops one at a time that many times. I wanted a volume I could pull with a syringe instead.

So I asked AI first. It came back with half a teaspoon per jug, linking a blog post someone had written working through the same problem. It read fine, had a source attached, I almost just went with it.

I did the math myself anyway. I have an engineering degree, and converting between units was something like half of what that degree actually was, so this is exactly the kind of check I already know how to run. The bottle also gives 22 mL per 55 gallons, which works out to 0.4 mL per gallon, so 1.4 mL for a 3.5 gallon jug. A standard drop is 0.05 mL, and at that rate 8 drops per gallon is also 0.4 mL, and 28 drops a jug is also 1.4 mL. The two sets of directions printed on the same bottle agree with each other exactly, which told me I was reading the label right before I'd even gotten to comparing it against anything else.

Converting 1.4 mL to teaspoons, at 0.203 teaspoons per mL, gives 0.284 teaspoons, just over a quarter teaspoon. Half a teaspoon is 2.46 mL, about 1.76 times the actual dose, close enough to double that I'd call it double. In drops, the blog and the AI were pointing me at something like 49 drops a jug where the bottle says 28.

Something similar came up at work not long ago. Three of us ran the same analysis with AI and got three different answers back. All three landed on the same overall sentiment, which is what makes it easy to miss, because any one of us working alone would have finished up thinking we had the answer and been roughly right. Fine for a summary. Not fine for a dose you're going to seal into a jug and maybe not open again for years.

I don't do anything clever about this now. I don't ask AI to double check its own math, and I don't trust it more because a source is attached. I do the arithmetic myself, and where I can, I look for a second path to the same number, the way the drops and the mL checked each other on that bottle. It took maybe two minutes longer than just going with the blog post, and it caught something that would have been very hard to notice was wrong until it already was.

If you're doing this yourself, go by the label on your own bottle, not by my numbers. Different products are apparently concentrated differently, and these are specific to what I bought. I still don't actually know what's in this stuff that makes water keep for years instead of months, that's still sitting on my list to go read about.
