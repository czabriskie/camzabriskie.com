---
title: What actually goes obsolete
description: Elon Musk says money stops mattering once AI makes everything abundant, and I don't buy that part, but I think I've seen a small real version of it happen to app stores.
date: 2026-09-07T12:00:00Z
---

Elon Musk has said a few times, on Rogan and in a couple of other interviews, that once AI and robots are producing goods and services at some huge scale, scarcity mostly goes away and money stops mattering the way it does now, he's put a date on it and talked about something he calls universal high income instead of universal basic income, and he points to those Iain Banks novels, the Culture books, as the kind of post-scarcity world he means. It's the economic argument I'm interested in here, nothing else that tends to come up around him.

I don't think money goes away, not while our social structures are set up the way they are, and plenty of people have pushed back on the scarcity premise itself too, so I'm not alone in being skeptical. But I think I've sort of seen into a small, real version of this, in exactly one narrow place, and it wasn't money disappearing at all.

I needed a tool. A game my kid could actually play that wasn't quietly built to squeeze her for a video ad or a purchase every few minutes, and I looked around for one for a while and came up with nothing, every option had the same problem underneath it somewhere. And then building it myself turned out to be faster than continuing to shop for it, and I mean that plainly, it cost me a night. That's what abundance actually felt like when it showed up for me, and it wasn't money going away, it was the store stopping being the place you go to get the thing.

My prediction is a lot narrower than Musk's. I think the app store as a destination is what goes away, not money. If you need a tool you can just build it, pretty quickly, and I think the store models as they exist right now are going to have to change fast to stay relevant, or people just stop going through them at all.

I didn't know this until I went looking, but it has already started happening in a regulated way, and the result so far mostly argues against me. The EU's Digital Markets Act, a law aimed at forcing large platforms to open up, came into force in March 2024 and requires Apple to allow other app marketplaces and sideloading, meaning installing an app without going through Apple's own store, on iPhones in Europe. AltStore PAL got approved as a marketplace that April, the Epic Games Store showed up on iOS in the EU that August with Fortnite in tow, Japan followed in December 2025 under its own Mobile Software Competition Act, and Brazil after that, out of an antitrust settlement there. Apple still notarizes anything distributed outside its store, which just means it scans the app first, so it's not like this is unsupervised territory even where it's allowed. And separately, in the US, a court forced Apple last year to let apps link out to their own websites for payment instead of Apple's system, that ruling got upheld on appeal with the remedy softened toward some "reasonable" commission Apple can still charge, and Apple's now floated charging developers up to 15% on those external links, with a judge still expected to weigh in. So the door has actually opened, multiple times, in multiple places. And from what I can tell, most ordinary iPhone owners just didn't walk through it. I saw one piece with a headline along the lines of "sideloading changed everything and nothing about my iPhone," and that's about right, the capability landed and hardly anyone's day changed.

Which, if I'm being honest with myself, is a real problem for my prediction. But I think the reason it didn't change anything is that a different store is still a store, and nobody was actually asking for a different store. I think people want to put their own thing on their own device, and that's a different question entirely, and it's where the two platforms still differ enormously today, way more than the marketplace stuff suggests. I built the rhythm game on Android, and getting my own build onto my own phone is genuinely five minutes: CI spits out an installable file, I copy it over, I've already allowed installs from outside the store, and it just sits there and keeps working, update after update. Doing that same thing on an iPhone, outside those regulated countries, means opening Xcode on a Mac and signing in with an Apple account, and if you're not paying, the free option Apple gives you expires the permission to run your own app after seven days. Apple doesn't document that clearly anywhere I could find, it's just a thing every iOS developer runs into and passes along. So a tool you built for yourself just stops working every week, on a clock, unless you plug the phone back in and re-sign it, forever, or you pay $99 a year for the version where that friction goes away. That's the concrete version of what I'm actually predicting, not the store, the seven-day leash on running your own code on your own device, and I'd bet that lands hardest on exactly the people most likely to build something for themselves in the first place.

I'm hedging on one piece of this, and I can at least say why, because I've lived both sides of it across these two projects. The rhythm game makes no network calls at all, I own every single part of it, nothing outside it can ever be turned off on me. The other game, the Spotify one, depended on somebody else's data and somebody else's endpoint, and the best feature in it, the one that jumped straight to the recognizable part of a song instead of the intro, ran on an audio analysis endpoint that Spotify just stopped serving to new apps like mine. So I don't think the honest prediction is that gatekeeping goes away, I think it's that it moves, off the store shelf and onto the API, and that's a transfer, not an abolition. I'm genuinely not sure about this part, there might be enough real complexity in how integrations get built and maintained that it doesn't play out this cleanly, but I trust it less than anything else here and I think about it more than the rest.

Money isn't going anywhere that I can see. But I've already stopped needing the store, at least for what I actually needed it for, and that happened quietly, one small tool at a time, without anyone announcing it.
