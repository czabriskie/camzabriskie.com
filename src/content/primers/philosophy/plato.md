Plato lived in Athens from about 428 to 347 BCE. He was Socrates' student and Aristotle's teacher [@iep-plato]. The [last primer](/primers/philosophy/sophists-and-socrates/) ended with Socrates drinking hemlock in 399 BCE. Plato watched that happen, and most of what we know about Socrates comes from what Plato wrote afterward [@pt-004, @sep-plato].

This primer follows episode 4 of *Philosophize This!* [@pt-004] and checks it against the Stanford and Internet Encyclopedias of Philosophy and against Plato's own text, the same way the [Presocratics primer](/primers/philosophy/presocratics/#which-source-to-trust) does. It covers four things:

1. **Why Plato is so well known.** He was a great writer, and that matters as much as his ideas.
2. **Why Socrates is in almost everything he wrote.**
3. **Platonic love.** Two speeches from the same dinner party: one says love is looking for the other half you lost, and the other says love is a climb toward beauty itself.
4. **The allegory of the cave and the world of Forms.** Plato's picture of what's really real and how we could ever know it.

The episode also gets into Plato's politics, which this primer leaves for another time.

A few terms come up throughout:

- **Dialogue.** Plato wrote philosophy as conversations between characters, like scripts. Each one is called a dialogue and usually named after a character, like the *Meno* or the *Phaedo* [@sep-plato].
- **The *Symposium*** and **the *Republic*** are the two dialogues this primer leans on most. The *Symposium* is about love. The *Republic* is about justice, and the cave is in it [@sep-plato, @iep-plato].
- **Form** (capital F, also translated **Idea**). Plato's name for the single perfect, unchanging thing that many imperfect things have in common. Lots of things are beautiful, and the Form of Beauty is beauty itself, the thing they all share [@sep-plato]. A Form is not a shape here. The section on [the world of Forms](#the-world-of-forms) explains it properly.
- ***Eros*** is the Greek word for passionate or romantic love, usually translated "love." It's where "erotic" comes from [@sep-plato-friendship].

## Plato could write

The podcast opens with Alfred North Whitehead's line that all of Western philosophy after Plato is a set of footnotes to Plato [@pt-004]. Part of the reason is that Plato's books survived and his teachers' didn't. Socrates wrote nothing, and the [Presocratics](/primers/philosophy/presocratics/) survive only as fragments other people quoted. Plato's dialogues came down to us intact [@sep-plato].

The Stanford encyclopedia calls Plato "one of the most dazzling writers in the Western literary tradition," and says the subject of philosophy, as people usually think of it, "can be called his invention" [@sep-plato]. The podcast says many historians rank his writing above Homer's [@pt-004]. That's a judgment call rather than a fact, but nobody disputes that he could write. An old story says he started out writing tragedies for the theater and burned one after hearing Socrates talk. Nobody knows if that's true, but the Internet Encyclopedia says there's no question of his "mastery of dialogue, characterization, and dramatic context" [@iep-plato].

The dialogues read like short plays. Most open somewhere specific, like a prison, a rich man's house, a drinking party, or a walk outside the city walls on a hot day. The people talking are mostly real Athenians with real reputations, and they have personalities [@sep-plato]. A conversation starts out about something ordinary and drifts into a hard question about justice or love or knowledge [@pt-004].

Plato never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues, so he never just tells you what to believe [@sep-plato]. Characters make arguments, other characters push back, and plenty of the dialogues end without an answer. The encyclopedia says this draws readers into thinking the problem through for themselves, and that the characters and conversation make the ideas interesting enough to reach a wider audience. Between that and the quality of the writing, Plato is often thought of as the ideal author to start philosophy with [@sep-plato].

## Socrates in nearly every dialogue

Socrates is the main speaker in most of Plato's dialogues. He's completely missing from only one, the *Laws*, which was one of the last things Plato wrote. In a few other late ones, like the *Sophist*, the *Statesman*, and the *Timaeus*, he's there but someone else does most of the talking [@sep-plato].

The podcast's explanation is that Plato was deliberately typecasting Socrates as the wise man in every story, the way an actor gets known for one kind of role, so that Socrates would be remembered that way [@pt-004]. Plato did have reasons to want that. Athens had executed Socrates as a corrupter of the young, and Plato's account of Socrates' relationships with young men in the *Symposium* reads as a defense of him [@sep-plato-friendship]. Plato called Socrates "the justest man alive," or at least the *Seventh Letter* does, and scholars disagree about whether Plato wrote it [@iep-plato, @sep-plato].

The encyclopedias add two things the podcast leaves out:

- **Plato wasn't the only one.** After Socrates died, several of his followers wrote conversations starring him. Xenophon's survive, and fragments of others do. Writing "Socratic discourses" was a genre, and Plato was arguing with the other writers about who Socrates really was [@sep-plato, @iep-plato].
- **Socrates turns into Plato.** Scholars split the dialogues into early, middle, and late periods. In the early ones Socrates asks questions about ethics, says he doesn't know the answers, and leaves the other person stuck, which fits what we know of the real man. In the middle ones a character still called Socrates starts confidently teaching new theories about the soul and the Forms. Those are Plato's ideas. He kept the name, and the encyclopedia says that let him show his debt to his teacher and borrow "the extraordinary prestige of the man who was the wisest of his time" [@sep-plato, @iep-plato].

So the podcast's point about immortalizing Socrates holds up, and it came at a cost. Making Socrates speak for Plato means we often can't tell where Socrates ends and Plato begins, the same Socratic problem the [last primer](/primers/philosophy/sophists-and-socrates/#who-was-he-really) ran into [@pt-004, @sep-plato]. The podcast doesn't think Socrates would have minded, since he never claimed to have fixed beliefs anyway [@pt-004].

## Platonic love

The *Symposium* is set at a drinking party, which is what the word means. After dinner the guests take turns giving speeches about love [@pt-004, @sep-plato]. Seven people speak [@sep-plato-friendship]. Two of the speeches are the famous ones, and they disagree.

Most of the speeches, Diotima's included, have in mind love between an older man and a younger one, a relationship Athenian custom had its own rules for, called *paiderastia* [@sep-plato-friendship]. Both speeches below still have something to say about love in general, and the first one explains why some people love men and others love women.

### Aristophanes: looking for your other half

Aristophanes, the comic playwright whose *Clouds* had mocked Socrates [@sep-plato], tells a myth that is half joke and half serious [@pt-004, @plato-symposium]:

1. Humans used to be round. Each had four arms, four legs, and one head with two faces looking opposite ways, and moved by cartwheeling. (The podcast says two heads. The text says one head, two faces.)
2. There were three sexes: all-male, all-female, and half-male half-female. The male ones came from the sun, the female ones from the earth, and the mixed ones from the moon.
3. They were strong and proud enough to attack the gods. Zeus didn't want to wipe them out, because then nobody would make sacrifices to the gods. So he cut each one in half, which made them weaker and doubled the number of worshippers.
4. Ever since, each half has been looking for the other half it was cut from.

The three kinds of original people explain three kinds of attraction. Halves of the mixed people are drawn to the opposite sex, halves of the all-female people to women, and halves of the all-male people to men [@plato-symposium]. When two halves find each other they don't want to be apart for a moment, and they can't explain what they want from each other. If a god offered to weld them back into one person, they would say yes. Aristophanes sums it up: "the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love" [@plato-symposium].

On this view love is about getting back something that was already yours. The podcast puts it as a quest to fill in what you're missing [@pt-004]. It's also where we get the phrase "my other half" [@iep-plato].

### Socrates: climbing toward beauty

Socrates speaks later. He says he learned everything he knows about love from a woman named Diotima, from Mantinea, and repeats her lesson [@sep-plato-friendship, @plato-symposium]. She answers Aristophanes almost directly. People say lovers are looking for their other half, she says, but we don't love the half or the whole unless it's *good*. People will even have their own hands and feet cut off if those have gone bad [@plato-symposium]. You love something because it's good or beautiful, not because it used to belong to you [@pt-004].

From there she builds up a definition:

- **Love wants the good, forever.** Not just to have good things but to keep them always [@plato-symposium].
- **We can't have anything forever, because we die.** So love turns into a drive to make something that outlasts us, which Diotima calls "giving birth in beauty" [@sep-plato-friendship]. Some people have children. Others go after fame, or make something that lasts, the way Homer's poems and Solon's laws did [@plato-symposium]. The podcast compares these achievements to a peacock's feathers [@pt-004].
- **Beautiful people are where this starts, not where it ends.** Loving a beautiful person is the first rung of a ladder.

The ladder goes like this [@plato-symposium, @sep-plato-friendship]:

1. **One beautiful body.** You fall for one person.
2. **All beautiful bodies.** You notice that this person's beauty is the same kind of thing as other people's beauty, and the obsession with one body relaxes. The podcast says the hot person turns out to be "not as much of a rare Pokémon" [@pt-004].
3. **Beautiful souls.** You start to value a beautiful character over a beautiful body, enough to love a good person who isn't much to look at.
4. **Beautiful laws and ways of life**, the things that shape good character.
5. **Beautiful knowledge.** Every field of study, and the "vast sea of beauty" across all of them [@plato-symposium].
6. **Beauty itself.** At the top you see a beauty that never grows or fades, isn't beautiful in one way and ugly in another, and isn't the beauty *of* anything in particular. Everything else that's beautiful gets its beauty from it [@plato-symposium]. That's the Form of Beauty.

Diotima says this is the life most worth living [@plato-symposium]. Beauty itself can't leave you or die on you, the podcast points out, but it can't love you back either, because it's perfect and doesn't need anything [@pt-004].

One common reading is that you use people as steps and kick the ladder away once you're up. The encyclopedia argues against that. The lover still loves the person, just not obsessively and not only for their looks, so the beloved "will now still be loved" when their looks fade [@sep-plato-friendship].

### The two side by side

| | Aristophanes | Socrates (via Diotima) |
|---|---|---|
| What love is | Wanting to be whole again | Wanting the good, forever |
| What you're looking for | The one person who is your other half | Beauty, starting with one person and ending with beauty itself |
| Why it's that person | They used to be part of you | They're beautiful or good |
| Where it ends | Two halves together for life | Seeing the Form of Beauty |

### What "platonic" means now

Today "platonic" usually means a close relationship without sex. The dictionary keeps both senses: the everyday one, and "love conceived by Plato as ascending from passion for the individual to contemplation of the universal and ideal" [@mw-platonic-love]. Plato's version isn't sexless friendship. It starts with physical attraction and climbs past it. Plato did see sex as a degraded, wasteful use of love's power, though, and thought love only got what it really wanted through philosophy [@iep-plato].

## The world of Forms

The love speech ends at beauty itself, a perfect beauty that isn't in any one beautiful thing. That idea runs under nearly everything Plato wrote [@sep-plato].

It starts with Socrates' question from the [last primer](/primers/philosophy/sophists-and-socrates/#2-right-and-wrong-are-real): what *is* courage, or justice, or beauty? Socrates wanted whatever all just acts have in common. Aristotle says Socrates looked for that common thing but Plato **separated** it. Plato decided it couldn't be anything in the world around us, because everything around us keeps changing [@sep-plato-metaphysics].

The podcast uses trees [@pt-004]. No two trees are the same. They have different bark, different leaves, a knot here and a missing branch there, and you still recognize every one of them as a tree. They share some "tree-y-ness." (Plato used dogs, the podcast says.) Plato's claim is that this shared thing is real, more real than any single tree, and it's the Form.

Forms have a few features in common [@sep-plato, @iep-plato]:

- **One over many.** There are many beautiful things and one Beauty, many equal things and one Equality. The many things get their names and their qualities from the one.
- **Perfect.** Every beautiful thing is beautiful in some ways and not others, or for a while and then not. Beauty itself is just beautiful, completely and always.
- **Unchanging and eternal.** They aren't in space or time.
- **Known by thinking, not by the senses.** You can see a beautiful face. You can only think your way to Beauty.

The encyclopedia's list of Plato's most important Forms is abstract: goodness, beauty, equality, sameness, difference [@sep-plato]. Trees and dogs make easier examples, but these are the Forms Plato cared about most.

This also answers the Presocratics' big argument. Heraclitus said everything is always changing, and Parmenides said real things can't change at all [@pt-004]. Plato takes both. The world our senses show us changes constantly, and the Forms never change [@pt-004, @iep-plato].

<p class="bitgrid-caption">The fire's light passes over the carried figure and lands on the back wall. The prisoners can only face the wall, so the shadow is all they ever see.</p>

1. **The prisoners.** People have lived since childhood chained in an underground cave, legs and necks fixed so they can only look at the back wall.
2. **The shadows.** Behind them a fire burns. Between the fire and the prisoners is a low wall, and people walk along behind it holding up statues of people and animals, like puppeteers. The prisoners see only the shadows of those statues on the wall. They give the shadows names, and they think the shadows are the real things. Prisoners who get good at guessing which shadow comes next win honors from the others.
3. **Turning around.** One prisoner is freed and made to turn toward the fire. It hurts his eyes, the statues look less real to him than the shadows did, and he wants to go back to looking at the wall.
4. **Out of the cave.** He's dragged up a steep path into the daylight and is blinded at first. He gets used to it slowly. First he can look at shadows and reflections in water, then at the things themselves, then at the night sky, and finally at the sun.
5. **Going back down.** If he went back to his old seat, his eyes would be useless in the dark and he'd be bad at the shadow-guessing game. The other prisoners would laugh at him and decide the trip up had ruined his eyes, so nobody should ever go. They wouldn't just refuse to follow him. If anyone tried to unchain them and lead them up to the light, and they could get their hands on that person, they would kill him [@plato-republic].

The podcast's version is close, with two small differences [@pt-004]. It suggests the freed prisoner would probably just turn back to the wall, but in the text he gets dragged out whether he likes it or not. And it skips the last step. Plato wrote it two or three decades after Athens executed Socrates for questioning people [@plato-republic, @iep-plato].

### What each part stands for

Socrates explains the picture himself: the cave is the world we see, the fire is the sun, and the climb out is the soul rising to the world of thought, where the last and hardest thing to see is the Form of the Good [@plato-republic]. The encyclopedia lines the rest up with the passage just before it in the *Republic* [@sep-plato-metaphysics]:

| In the cave | Stands for |
|---|---|
| Shadows on the wall | Images and appearances, what most people take for reality |
| Statues carried behind the wall | The ordinary physical things our senses show us |
| Things outside the cave | The Forms |
| The sun | The Form of the Good, which makes everything else knowable |
| The climb out | The soul rising from what the senses show to what only thought can reach, slowly and painfully |

### After the climb

The podcast says Plato thought a philosopher's job is to work out the Forms of as many things as they can in their lifetime [@pt-004]. Each one changes how you see the everyday version. The podcast compares it to watching football next to a pro player. You're both watching the same game, but the player sees the strategy and the small details that you miss completely [@pt-004]. Plato thought the philosopher who has left the cave sees everyday life that way.

That also makes him hard to talk to. The prisoners only know the shadows, so when he talks about what's outside, they have nothing to compare it to. Plato puts him in court, still "blinking" and not used to the dark again, forced to argue "about the images or the shadows of images of justice" with people "who have never yet seen absolute justice" [@plato-republic]. He knows what justice really is and they don't, so they argue past each other, and he comes off looking ridiculous. Plato says that shouldn't surprise anyone. Laughing at someone whose eyes are still adjusting after coming down from the light makes less sense, he says, than laughing at someone who has never left the dark [@plato-republic].

Plato still doesn't let him stay outside. Later in Book VII, Socrates says the people who have made the climb can't be allowed to stay up there, however much they'd like to. They "must be made to descend again among the prisoners." Once their eyes adjust to the dark, they "will see ten thousand times better than the inhabitants of the den," because they know what each shadow is a shadow of [@plato-republic]. In the *Republic*, those people go back down to rule the city. The podcast calls that kind of ruler a philosopher king. It belongs to Plato's politics, which this primer leaves for another time [@pt-004].

So the duty runs both ways. Climb as high as you can, all the way to the Form of the Good, and then come back down and help the people who are still watching shadows, even if they don't want your help.

## How would anyone know a Form?

You can't see a Form, so how would you know when you'd reached one? Plato's answer, mainly in the *Meno*, is that you already knew it before you were born. The soul is immortal and saw the Forms before birth, and learning is really **recollection**, remembering what the soul already knew [@sep-plato, @iep-plato].

His evidence is a scene where Socrates questions a slave boy who has never studied geometry [@iep-plato]. Socrates draws a square and asks how to draw one with twice the area. The boy says to double the sides, which is wrong. Socrates never tells him the answer. He only asks questions, and the boy reaches new conclusions on his own [@pt-004]. For Plato, if nobody taught him, the boy must have been remembering.

The podcast calls this a breakthrough in **epistemology**, the study of what we know and how we know it, and credits Plato with laying the groundwork for the 17th-century rationalists, who also trusted reason over the senses [@pt-004].

## Did Plato mean a separate world literally?

The podcast mentions that a lot of smart people doubt Plato really believed in a separate world full of perfect things floating around. On that reading he was saying something more modest, that justice is something different from any particular just act [@pt-004].

The scholarship doesn't settle it. Aristotle, who studied with Plato, reported that Plato "separated" the Forms. Scholars still argue about what that means. It might mean only that you can't *define* Beauty by pointing at beautiful things, or it might mean the Forms really *exist* apart from everything else [@sep-plato-metaphysics]. Plato kept testing the theory himself. Another dialogue, the *Parmenides*, raises hard objections to the Forms and doesn't answer them, and the *Republic* calls the Form of the Good a mystery that nobody yet fully understands [@sep-plato].

Courage isn't the same thing as any one brave act, and you can be wrong about what's really beautiful or just, which means there's something there to be wrong about. Plato's answer to the Sophists' "man is the measure" was that the measure is outside us [@pt-004].
