Elon Musk has been making a version of this argument in a few places, including on Rogan: AI and robots produce so much that scarcity fades and money stops mattering, and his line is roughly that if food and housing and transport are that abundant, what exactly do you need money for. I don't buy the money part. As long as our social structures are arranged the way they are, money is doing a lot of jobs that abundance doesn't touch. But I think I've seen a small, real version of it in exactly one place, and it isn't the economy.

I needed a tool, a game my kid could play that wasn't built to squeeze her, and building it turned out to be faster than shopping for one. That's what the abundance actually felt like from the inside. Not money going away, just the store quietly stopping being the place I go to get the thing. So my narrower prediction is that the store as a destination is what goes obsolete, well before money does.

Except this has already been tested, and it mostly didn't happen. The EU's Digital Markets Act has required Apple to allow third-party app marketplaces and sideloading since 2024, real alternatives shipped, Epic put Fortnite back on iPhones there, Japan and Brazil followed. And most people just shrugged and kept using the App Store. Which makes sense to me now: a different store is still a store, and nobody was actually hungry for one.

The pressure comes from somewhere else, I think. It comes from people who want to put their own thing on their own device, and that's where the platforms still differ enormously. On Android I copied my build onto a phone and it stays there. On an iPhone, with a free account, the profile that lets your own app run expires after seven days, so something you made for yourself quietly dies every week unless you re-sign it or pay the yearly fee. That friction lands hardest on exactly the people most likely to build something, which seems like a strange group to charge admission.

I'm least sure about integrations, and I've got receipts on both sides from my own projects. The self-contained thing I own completely. The one that depended on somebody else's API lost its best feature when that endpoint got switched off. So maybe the gatekeeping doesn't end so much as move, off the store shelf and onto the API, which would be a transfer rather than an abolition.
